Stage 1 of 3

An embedding puts meaning on a map

An embedding model reads a word, sentence or document and returns a list of numbers. Treat that list as coordinates and every piece of text becomes a point. Things that mean similar things land near each other.

For engineers: what this cartoon leaves out
  • Real embeddings have 384 to 3072 dimensions, not 2. The axes are not named; a model learns directions that happen to correlate with things like gender or formality, and most directions have no clean human label.
  • The king − man + woman ≈ queen result comes from word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) and holds only approximately. Modern retrieval models embed whole sentences or passages in context, so "queen" in a chess document and "queen" in a history document get different vectors.
  • Nearness is usually measured by cosine similarity, not straight-line distance. Vectors are normalised to unit length, so the dot product is the score.
  • Finding the nearest points among millions is done with an approximate nearest-neighbour index (HNSW, IVF, ScaNN). It trades a little recall for queries in milliseconds instead of seconds.
cos(q, d) = (q · d) / (‖q‖ ‖d‖) → with unit vectors: score = Σᵢ qᵢ dᵢ

Stage 2 of 3

Two ways to turn one document into numbers

A sparse vector has one slot per word in the vocabulary and is almost entirely zeros. A dense vector has a few hundred slots and every one of them is filled. They are built differently and they find different things.

For engineers: how each vector is produced and stored

Sparse. Dimension = vocabulary size (30k to 100k+). Classic weights come from BM25: term frequency, damped and length-normalised, times inverse document frequency, so rare terms count more. Learned sparse models (SPLADE, BGE-M3 sparse) keep the same shape but let a transformer pick the weights and add related terms the document never said. Stored as an inverted index: term → posting list of documents and weights. Exact, explainable, cheap.

BM25(q, d) = Σ_{t∈q} idf(t) · tf(t,d)·(k₁+1) / (tf(t,d) + k₁·(1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl))

Dense. Dimension is fixed by the model (384, 768, 1024, 1536, 3072). A transformer encoder reads the passage, and the token outputs are pooled into one vector. No slot has a name; meaning is spread across all of them. Stored in a vector index (HNSW, IVF-PQ) and compared by cosine. Handles paraphrase, synonyms and cross-language queries, but is blind to tokens it never saw in training such as part numbers, new product names and internal jargon.

  • Sparse wins on exact identifiers, codes, names, rare jargon, and anything newer than the model.
  • Dense wins on intent, paraphrase, misspellings, and queries phrased nothing like the document.
  • Neither is strictly better. That is the whole reason for stage 3.

Stage 3 of 3

A hybrid index asks both and merges the answers

One query goes to two indexes. Each returns its own ranked list with its own kind of score. A fusion step turns the two lists into one. Documents that both indexes liked rise to the top, and documents only one index could find still make the list.

For engineers: fusion, scoring and where this runs

The two retrievers return scores on unrelated scales (cosine in [−1, 1], BM25 unbounded), so you cannot add them raw. Two standard fixes:

weighted: score = α · norm(cos) + (1 − α) · norm(bm25) norm = min–max over the returned list
RRF: score = Σ_lists 1 / (k + rank_in_list) k = 60 by convention
  • RRF ignores score magnitudes entirely, which makes it robust and tuning-free. Weighted fusion lets you bias toward one side, which is useful when you know your traffic is ID-heavy or question-heavy.
  • Most engines run both indexes in one place: Elasticsearch and OpenSearch (BM25 + kNN), Qdrant, Weaviate, Vespa, Postgres with tsvector + pgvector. Some return a fused list natively; otherwise fuse in application code.
  • Fetch more than you need from each side (say top 50 each) before fusing, or a document strong in one index and absent from the other's top-k never gets a chance.
  • A common third stage is a cross-encoder reranker over the fused top 20 to 100. It reads query and document together and is far more accurate, but too slow to run over the whole corpus.

The short version

Three sentences to take away

Dense

Meaning as a position. Finds things that are about the same thing, even in different words.

Sparse

Words as switches. Finds things that contain the exact term, including codes the model has never seen.

Hybrid

Ask both, merge the lists. Documents both agree on rise; documents only one could find still appear.
